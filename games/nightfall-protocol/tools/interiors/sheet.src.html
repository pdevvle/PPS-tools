Roofs and Houses

Reference for the interiors generator. It covers how roofs, wall build-ups, storeys and footings are chosen, so that each building gets one that fits where it stands, when it was built and the ground it sits on. Every table here is read from the generator's own data, and the roof drawings come from its roof code.

A1Roof forms

Each house takes one roof form. The generator covers the footprint with a few overlapping rectangles and puts the form on each one, all at the same pitch and eave height. Where they overlap, the roofs run into each other the way cross gables and hipped wings do on real houses. Forms that only suit a plain box (shed, butterfly, gambrel, saltbox, A-frame, Dutch gable) fall back to a gable or hip when the footprint has wings.

A2Roof coverings

The covering sets the pitch range and the look. On screen each covering is carried by tone in courses at least 0.25 m wide: shingle and slate courses run along the eave, tile ribs and metal seams run down the slope. No hairline joints, so nothing shimmers.

A3House types

Each type says how many storeys it has, which roofs and coverings it wears, what its walls are clad in, and which footprints it can sit on. A long 4:1 box reads as a single-wide; a Colonial needs a near-rectangle. The footprint comes from the map, so the type has to fit it, not the other way round.

A5Frames and walls

A6Storeys and footings

Basements follow the frost line. Where footings must go 0.9 to 1.2 m down (the Midwest, the Northeast), digging a full basement costs little more, so most houses have one. In Arizona the frost line is shallow and the ground is caliche and rock, so almost no house does: slab on grade is the rule. Hillside lots change that everywhere. Where the ground falls a storey under the house, the downhill half becomes a walk-out lower floor and the rest stands on posts.

A7How a house is matched

  1. Read the footprint. Its area, how long and how boxy it is, and how many rectangles make it up.
  2. Read the lot. Ground heights from the terrain data under every cell give the fall across the house and which way is downhill. The panel can override this with a level lot or a steep one.
  3. Pick the era from the region's weights.
  4. Pick the type. Region weight × fits the footprint × built in that era (a type out of its era keeps a fifth of its weight) × slope (hillside types gain on steep lots). A clean long box strongly suggests a manufactured home.
  5. Dress it. Storeys, roof form and covering, pitch (within both the type's and the covering's range), eave depth, cladding, colours and porch, all from the type's tables for the region.
  6. Stand it on the ground. The footing comes from the type or the region, then the lot's fall overrides it. The main floor sits on the highest ground under it plus the footing's lift. Slabs get a levelled pad; raised floors keep the slope beneath them.
  7. Plan the floors from their own masks: a basement or lower floor gets family room, storage, a guest room and bath; the main floor gets the public rooms and the garage; bedrooms go up. Walls with earth against them get no doors or windows, except small egress windows high up in a basement bedroom.

Seeded: the same building, region and lot always give the same house. Changing the region re-rolls the design, because a Northeast house on this footprint is a different house.

A8Importing a real home

This is the route for hand-crafted places. A real listing gives the facts and photos; the map gives the footprint and the ground; a short layout file puts them together. The generator then furnishes it and stocks loot as it does for every other building.

  1. Find it on the map. The listing's coordinates point to an OSM building. For 48918 N Fig Springs Rd, New River, that is way 1046267351, a D-shaped 398 m² outline traced from aerial imagery. It includes the deck: 2,743 sq ft of living space plus the 1,400 sq ft deck comes to about 385 m².
  2. Fetch the site. import_home.py caches the footprint, drives within 120 m and real ground (Terrarium tiles, 4 m grid). Here the ground falls about 2.8 m from the back to the curved front.
  3. Write the layout from the listing and photos: storeys, rooms as zones in metres (boxes and inside or outside circles), doors, window walls, a deck, and the stair. Fig Springs: a garage level of reinforced block under the house (4-car garage, full bath, walk-in vault) and a main floor whose curved half is a great room behind a wall of windows. Kitchen and dining are on the west curve by the French doors. Bedrooms, the study and the master suite run along the straight north wall.
  4. Design facts become the design: stucco over 6 in frame, a foam flat roof with a deep overhang, a walk-out lower floor.
  5. The generator lays it on the cells, places the listed doors and window walls, links anything left unjoined, furnishes and stocks it, and runs the gameplay passes: a second way into every big room, a cover budget, room-sized clutter.
  6. Adapt for play in the layout file: widen a hall, add a back stair, move a door. The listing is the start, not a constraint.

The photos are looked at for reference only and are not copied into the game or the repo. Positions inside are inferred from what each photo shows next to what, so they are approximate. The 1 m grid turns the curved front into steps; true curved walls are on the list below.
